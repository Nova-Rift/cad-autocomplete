How this was made

1

Free drawings

9.2 million sketches

Real sketches people drew in a design tool, collected by Princeton researchers (SketchGraphs). Each one lists its shapes in the order they were drawn.

Lesson: nobody marked anything by hand. The shape a person drew next is the right answer.

2

Drawings become numbers

line 12 16 52 16

A model only reads numbers. Each shape becomes its kind (line, circle, arc, point) plus its positions on a 64 × 64 grid. A sketch becomes a sentence, and the job becomes guessing the next word.

3

Cleaning

9.2 → 2.5 million

Most sketches were copies of each other, so only one of each was kept. Over a third of the test sketches also sat in the training pile and were taken out of the test.

Lesson: a test the model has already seen flatters the score. Keep the test pile honest.

4

The model

4.8 million weights

A small model of the same family as chat AIs, written from scratch. It reads the shapes so far and scores every possible next number.

5

Training

1 rented GPU

The model guesses, gets corrected, and its weights are nudged, millions of times. Weight decay keeps the weights small, and the copy that does best on unseen sketches is the one kept.

Lesson: watch two scores. A growing gap between seen and unseen sketches means memorising.

6

Scoring

still training

When training ends, the model is tested on sketches it never saw: hide the next shape, ask for a guess, count how often it is right.

Lesson: not just accuracy. Of the guesses shown, how many were right (precision)? Of all steps, how many got a right guess (recall)?

7

Into the browser

0.13 seconds a guess

The model was rewritten in plain JavaScript so it runs on this page with no server. It was checked against the GPU version and gives the same guesses.

⚙

The settings used

Learning rate 0.001 (eased in over the first 1,000 steps, then slowly lowered) · 1,024 sketches per step · weight decay 0.05 · no dropout · 60,000 steps, about 24 passes over the data · 6 layers, 256 wide, 4 heads.

Honest note: these were first picks from experience and they worked first time. Nothing was tuned yet, so there is likely a better score to find.

!

What did not work at first

Too many helpers. The cleaning step ran 96 helper programs on a machine with about 18 processors. They tripped over each other and it looked frozen. With 16 it took two minutes.

A silent wait. That first run printed nothing, so there was no telling stuck from slow. It was restarted with progress messages.

Missing tools. The rented machine lacked the file-copy tool and parts of the data reader; each was installed by hand.

Lesson: make every long job say what it is doing.

The same project, as an interview book's five parts

This was built while studying Inside the Machine Learning Interview by Peng Shao. Its five parts, and where each one shows up here.

1

The basics

Telling memorising from too weak by comparing two scores. Keeping the test pile honest. Precision and recall instead of plain accuracy. Weight decay.

Here: cleaning, training and scoring (steps 3, 5, 6).

2

Coding

Writing the pieces yourself rather than calling a library: the model, the training loop, the search for the best few guesses.

Here: the model is written out twice, once for the GPU and once in plain JavaScript for this page.

4

Infrastructure

The machinery around the model: where it trains, where its saved copies go, how it reaches people.

Here: one rented GPU with a spending cap, saved copies sent to cloud storage every 2,000 steps, and a check that the page's model matches the GPU's.

5

Advanced questions

What goes wrong in real systems. The big one here: the model was trained on sketches that always fill the page, but people do not draw that way.

Here: training sketches are now shrunk and shifted at random so practice looks like real use.
